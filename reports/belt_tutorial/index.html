ThinkBooster tutorial · BELT, belief-based termination

Watching a reasoning model decide when it is done

BELT: Ni, Abulkhanov, Smirnov, Leippold, Ash, Baldwin, Shelmanov and Sachan, Same Beliefs, Different Policies: Learning Reusable Representations for Early Stopping in LLM Reasoning, arXiv preprint, 2026 · code at github.com/EdisonNi-hku/BELT
ThinkBooster: Smirnov et al., ThinkBooster: A Unified Framework for Seamless Test-Time Scaling of LLM Reasoning, arXiv:2606.06915 · code at github.com/IINemo/thinkbooster

BELT reads a frozen model's hidden states at every reasoning boundary and keeps four beliefs about the answer: is it right now, will it be right at the end, will a right answer still come, and will the current answer survive. A small linear policy turns those beliefs into a stop decision. In ThinkBooster, BELT is one more test-time strategy: a config switch adds it to a benchmark run, and the same engine deploys on vLLM behind an OpenAI-compatible API, so the model really generates fewer reasoning tokens. Every curve below comes from real ThinkBooster runs.

Beyond about 30% savings, a plain token cap does as well in our one-sample-per-problem setup. On Minerva Math, outside the probe's training domain, the gains are small.

Four beliefs, one probe

Stopping is not only "is the answer right?". A wrong answer may be about to get fixed, and a right one may still be overturned. BELT trains one 16M-parameter probe on the model's layer-16 states to predict four outcomes at every block end, independent of any token price.

Now
If the model answered right now, would it be correct?
Yinow = yi
End
If it thinks until it stops by itself, will it be correct?
Yiend = yT
Later
Will a correct answer appear at some later boundary?
Yilater = 1{∃ j>i : yj = 1}
Same
Is the current answer the one it will end with?
Yisame = 1{ai = aT}

A block is one reasoning step: text up to a blank line. At block i, ai is the answer the model gives if forced to answer there, yi = 1 if that answer is correct, and T is the block where the model stops by itself.

BELT inside ThinkBooster

ThinkBooster runs test-time strategies (best-of-n, beam search, self-consistency and others) over one generation engine, one set of benchmarks and one evaluator. BELT plugs in as a strategy plus a scorer, so it is run, logged and graded exactly like the other strategies, and served like them (see "Deploy it" below). One loop per problem:

  1. 1 · Generate a blockVLLMStepGeneratorvLLM writes reasoning until the next blank line (one BELT block).
  2. 2 · Read the beliefsStepScorerBELTEvery 4th block (4 to 128): layer-16 states of the prefix go through the probe, giving Now, End, Later, Same and a 768-d representation. Backend: hf or vllm.
  3. 3 · DecideBeltStoppingPolicyA linear stop score from the beliefs (BELT-B) or the representation (BELT-H). Below the threshold: back to step 1.
  4. 4 · Answer and gradeStrategyBELTClose thinking with </think>, generate the answer, grade it with ThinkBooster's evaluator. Every decision's beliefs and score are saved with the result.

Belief timeline

A decision point is every fourth block end, from block 4 to block 128. There BELT reads the four beliefs and a stopping policy computes a stop score, its log-odds of stopping; BELT stops at the first decision point where the score reaches the threshold. The paper fits two kinds of policy: BELT-B reads only the four beliefs and the prefix length, and BELT-H reads the probe's full 768-d state, from which the beliefs are computed. The stop score drawn here is BELT-H, the stronger of the two; BELT-B is in the next section.

To check the beliefs against reality, we also forced an answer at every decision point (close thinking, ask for \boxed{…}) and graded it. Filled dots are correct forced answers.

Same beliefs, different policies

The beliefs do not depend on how much a token costs. The price lives only in the policy, a logistic layer refit on CPU in seconds. The paper fits each policy to maximize accuracy minus λ × tokens, so λ says how many points of accuracy a token is worth. Slide it to see how the paper's 17 fitted BELT-B policies (four beliefs plus prefix length) reweight the same inputs.

Why a plain token cap beats BELT at large savings on MATH-500

Saving tokens means cutting long runs, and long runs are often wrong. The longest quarter of MATH-500 runs uses 65% of all reasoning tokens and is right 70% of the time; runs shorter than the median (1,599 tokens) are right 96% of the time.

The cap cuts only those long runs; BELT-H also stops many short ones. Where each method stops at a 30% token saving:

BELT picks better moments, but rarely becomes confident on long, hard runs. Within the long runs it loses 15 problems in 74 stops, against 26 in 112 for the cap. But its peak stop score is typically −2.4 on the longest runs and +7.0 on short ones: on hard problems the beliefs keep hoping a correct answer will still come. To save 30%, the threshold has to drop to about −5, far below the policy's fitted boundary of 0, and at that threshold BELT also stops 192 short runs early. They give only 15% of its saving and cost 11 problems. At its own threshold of 0, BELT-H is fine: 18% fewer tokens for 2.2 points of accuracy.

Adding a cap to BELT fixes its weakness at large savings. Keep BELT-H at its fitted threshold of 0 and also stop any run that passes a token cap: BELT handles the moments it is confident about, and the cap cuts the long runs where it never is. Like the cap alone, the rule has one setting, the cap. To keep the comparison fair, each method's setting is picked on a random half of the problems and scored on the other half, over 500 such splits:

Two backends: Hugging Face and vLLM

The probe needs the model's layer-16 hidden states for the whole prefix at every decision. ThinkBooster can get them two ways. The Hugging Face backend is the default and the one behind every number above; the vLLM backend reads the states from the engine that is already generating the text. Same probe, same policy, same decisions to within bf16 noise.

The HF cost grows with prefix length because it re-reads the whole prefix each time; vLLM's stays flatter. vLLM was timed in a separate engine that had not generated these prefixes, so every query paid a prefill; in a live run the states are captured during generation, so these vLLM times are an upper bound. Belief differences between the backends: median 0.005, 99th percentile 0.041. In a live run the vLLM backend's queries are almost free (the states were captured while generating), yet the whole run is not faster yet: it generates in eager mode, without CUDA graphs (820 s against about 700 s of generation for HF), and takes at most 9 problems at a time. For a 1.7B model scoring is a small share of the time, so today the HF backend with a large batch is the fastest setup. The vLLM backend pays off where re-reading the prefix dominates: longer prefixes, larger models, more frequent decisions. Wall times include model loading; the 64-problem accuracies differ by one problem.

Deploy it: a vLLM model that thinks less

In deployment BELT sits inside the generation loop. vLLM writes the reasoning block by block; every fourth block BELT reads the beliefs and the policy decides. A stop closes </think> and the model writes its final answer, so the rest of the reasoning is never generated: the saving is real compute, not a post-hoc cut. BeltEngine loads the model, the probe and the policy once; ThinkBooster's OpenAI-compatible service exposes the same engine to any client.

3 · Deploy with vLLM in Python

from thinkbooster.belt.engine import BeltEngine

# Qwen3-1.7B in vLLM + the BELT probe + the stopping policy, loaded once.
engine = BeltEngine("belt-policy.json", backend="hf")   # or backend="vllm"

for out in engine.generate(["Find the remainder when 2^100 is divided by 7."]):
    print(out.answer)             # final \boxed{} answer
    print(out.thinking_tokens)    # reasoning tokens actually generated
    print(out.stopped_early, out.stop_decision)   # did BELT close </think>, and where

How these numbers were made

  • One sampled trajectory per problem (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, seed 1), generated block by block in ThinkBooster with vLLM; BELT scored with the HF backend at every fourth block from block 4 up to block 128.
  • Natural completion is the model's own answer after it closes thinking. A stop at a decision point uses the forced answer there: </think>\n\n\boxed{ plus up to 48 tokens, graded with ThinkBooster's exact-match evaluator. Token counts are reasoning tokens plus the boxed answer; BELT's own scoring compute is not included.
  • The paper reads its curves off eight chains per question, filtered integer-answer sets and interpolation between operating points. With one chain per question, "0 points of tolerance" means not losing a single problem, so our readings are coarser and lower than the paper's ~41% out-of-distribution saving.
  • BELT-H uses the paper's seed-0 BELT-H-R fit at λ = 1.15 × 10⁻⁵ as one direction, with the threshold moved (the paper's recommended H-T form). Policies are applied deterministically (stop when score ≥ threshold); the paper fits and reads them as stochastic policies.
